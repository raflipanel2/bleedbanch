COLOR & TYPE

Paper stock and finish: why the same artwork prints differently

Understand how coating, opacity, texture and finish affect the result.

BY BLEEDBENCH EDITORIAL09 NOV 2026~3 MIN READ

A design proof on a glowing display does not reveal paper texture, opacity, surface reflectivity or ink absorption. Paper choice changes how colors, type and photographs appear. Even a file with correct color profiles and generous resolution may look different on matte uncoated paper versus a high-gloss coated sheet.

Coated and uncoated surfaces

Coated stock generally controls ink spread and can preserve crisp detail and strong color differently from absorbent uncoated papers. Uncoated textures may create a softer look that suits stationery and editorial work.

Neither finish is universally better. Choose based on the message, writing needs, glare, tactile feel and the print process available.

Thickness and opacity matter

Two papers with similar apparent thickness can differ in stiffness and show-through. For double-sided documents, test whether dark imagery on the back distracts from front-side text.

Ask about stock specification rather than assuming gsm alone determines opacity or feel. Paper bulk, finish and fiber construction affect the result.

Consider ink coverage

Large solid dark areas may behave differently on coated and uncoated stock. Fine detail may lose definition, and drying or finish conditions can affect handling.

Request a sample on the actual stock if color, tactile quality or dense coverage is commercially important.

Plan for folds and grain

Paper grain direction can influence how a sheet folds and how a booklet lies. Thick stock may crack at a fold if scoring or other finishing is not used.

A print provider can advise whether scoring, creasing or grain alignment is required. Do not rely on a screen mockup to evaluate folding behavior.

Choose finishing with the content in mind

Matte lamination can reduce glare but change perceived contrast. Gloss coatings can increase apparent vibrancy but attract reflections. Spot UV, foil and embossing introduce registration and minimum feature requirements.

Ask for the shop’s finishing template and actual material swatches before using a decorative effect in important type or a logo.

The same design on two stocks

Picture a postcard printed first on coated gloss stock and then on a soft, uncoated stock. The photo may appear punchier on the coated surface and more subdued on the uncoated one. The light reflecting from each surface changes perceived contrast, and the print process may need different color handling.

Rather than expecting identical appearance, select stock as part of the design brief. Ask the vendor for samples and discuss whether color matching or visual character matters more. Approve a proof on the intended substrate whenever the difference is important.

Opacity and folds are easy to overlook

A flyer printed on both sides may look clean as separate PDFs but become hard to read when dark reverse-side imagery shows through. A thick cardstock cover may crack when folded without scoring, especially across heavy ink coverage. These are physical production issues not visible in a pixel-perfect mockup.

Test opacity by holding a sample to typical viewing light. Ask about grain direction, scoring and crease methods before placing large solid graphics across a fold. Compare physical results, not just nominal grams per square meter.

Watch for this mistake

A color approval on one stock should not be assumed to apply identically to a completely different paper.

Before you send the file

  • Ask for physical swatches of the intended stock.
  • Check opacity and two-sided show-through.
  • Confirm folding, scoring and grain needs.
  • Proof special finishes at actual size.

Where to verify the details

Production requirements differ among print providers. Check the specific job template and consult the BleedBench reference directory for manufacturer documentation on page boxes, bleed, effective PPI and PDF output. If a client or shop gives a different specification, obtain clarification before export.
