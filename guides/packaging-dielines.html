Packaging artwork: dielines, folds and production layers

A clear approach to packaging layouts that prevents printing onto the wrong panels.

BY BLEEDBENCH EDITORIAL09 NOV 2026~3 MIN READ

Packaging is three-dimensional manufacturing described by a two-dimensional file. A carton dieline contains cut edges, folds, flaps and glue areas that may look deceptively similar on-screen. Good artwork respects what happens after cutting and folding, not just the flat arrangement.

Use a confirmed dieline

Get a dieline from the actual packaging manufacturer. Panel dimensions, flap sizes, tolerances and board thickness depend on its cutting and folding process.

Do not rebuild a complex packaging shape using guessed measurements. A 1 mm error near a lock tab or glue flap can create an assembly problem.

Label nonprinting production paths

Separate cut, crease, perforation and varnish layers according to the provider’s instructions. A visual key can help reviewers but production colors and path styles must follow the vendor’s specification.

Prevent guides from accidentally printing as artwork by using the required nonprinting or spot-color conventions. Check the final PDF in a separation preview.

Protect text near folds and seams

Text placed too near a crease may distort or become difficult to read when assembled. A glue flap needs appropriate unprinted space where required; coating or heavy ink can interfere with bonding on some materials.

Use an assembled paper dummy to see which panels are upright, which edges meet and which surfaces become hidden.

Consider substrate and finish

Uncoated board, coated board, corrugate and flexible films reproduce artwork differently. Fine details and dark colors can change appearance; ask for the relevant print process and color guidance.

Special finishes such as foil, varnish and embossing need accurately registered paths and may require minimum gap or stroke sizes. These are production-specific.

Approve both flat and assembled proofs

Check the flat sheet for production layers, bleed and quality, then check a folded prototype for reading order, accessibility of information and correct panel alignment.

A packaging file can be technically clean but still fail when assembled. Review the user’s interaction with the finished object before release.

How a flat carton becomes a box

Imagine a folding carton with four visible wall panels, a glue flap and top/bottom closures. The artboard shows the entire net in a line, but the customer sees the object folded into a box. A logo positioned on the wrong side of the flat file can end up on an interior flap or upside down after assembly.

Print the dieline on ordinary paper, cut and fold a dummy without adhesive first. Mark each visible face and verify orientation. A paper model is especially helpful when the packaging includes a window, hanger tab or asymmetric locking flaps.

The difference between guides and ink

A packaging file may contain spot-color lines named CUT, CREASE and VARNISH. Those are production instructions, not part of the decorative artwork. The exact names and layers must follow the converter’s workflow. If the final PDF merges them into normal printed color, the cut path may appear on finished packaging.

Keep production paths separate and inspect separations in the exported PDF. Confirm any glue-free zones, coatings, board grain and minimum line widths with the manufacturer. Never infer those tolerances from a generic online dieline.

Watch for this mistake

Artwork that looks centered on a flat dieline may be off-center after folding if the visible panel dimensions were misunderstood.

Before you send the file

  • Use the manufacturer’s approved dieline.
  • Separate cut, fold and finishing paths from artwork.
  • Protect text near creases and adhesive flaps.
  • Inspect an assembled sample before volume production.

Where to verify the details

Production requirements differ among print providers. Check the specific job template and consult the BleedBench reference directory for manufacturer documentation on page boxes, bleed, effective PPI and PDF output. If a client or shop gives a different specification, obtain clarification before export.
