Handing files to a printer: questions to ask before export

A clear intake checklist that reduces guesswork and last-minute fixes.

BY BLEEDBENCH EDITORIAL09 NOV 2026~3 MIN READ

The most useful prepress tool is sometimes a short email. Asking a print shop for its exact specifications saves rounds of re-exporting and avoids guessing about bleed, color profiles, marks and material finish. This guide organizes those questions so a designer can get a complete production brief before creating the final PDF.

Ask about the finished product

Specify item type, finished dimensions, quantity, printing sides, material and finishing. A postcard with rounded corners and a two-sided brochure use different practical safe areas even if both are printed on paper.

Find out whether the provider supplies an approved template or dieline. If it does, start there rather than constructing your own geometry from memory.

Request the technical submission format

Ask which PDF preset or standard is preferred, whether they want single pages or spreads, whether crop marks are required, and what bleed and safe margins to use.

The answers depend on the shop and the job. Write them down in the project file so another designer does not unknowingly override them later.

Clarify image and color requirements

Ask whether the provider accepts tagged RGB content, expects a CMYK profile, supports spot colors and has a recommended image-resolution range.

If precise color matters, ask about proof options and the available paper/press combination. A generic RGB-to-CMYK conversion is not an agreement about output appearance.

Plan delivery and sign-off

Clarify file naming, upload method, maximum upload size, expected proof turnaround and how revisions are approved.

A multi-person production chain benefits from one approved version and one contact who can answer technical questions without guessing.

Confirm what the shop will handle

Printers often perform imposition, trimming and some preflight operations. Some online services expect artwork to follow rigid templates with no manual marks.

Ask what is included and what must be prepared by the designer. That boundary is more important than mastering every export checkbox.

The questions worth asking in one message

For an A5 flyer job, a useful production request includes: finished 148 × 210 mm size, quantity, one or two printed sides, intended paper stock, desired finish and delivery date. Then ask for bleed, safe inset, preferred PDF preset, crop mark policy, color profile and proof process. This lets the provider respond to an actual job rather than a vague question about “print-ready format.”

A concise written response is valuable because the same shop may use different processes for a poster, a sticker and a booklet. Save its advice with the working file. Do not confuse a generic page on the shop website with a custom quote for your specific product.

A checklist for the final email

When the PDF is ready, mention the exact file version, final size, number of pages or sides, bleed included and any special layers such as cut paths or spot finishes. State whether the file has been approved by the customer. Ask the vendor to flag nonconformities before production begins.

The goal is not to make the printer responsible for all design decisions. It is to make the handoff unambiguous. If the shop reports a mismatch, correct the source and export a new labeled revision rather than editing an already-approved PDF in place.

Watch for this mistake

The correct setup for one provider is not automatically correct for another.

Before you send the file

  • Confirm final dimensions, quantity, stock and finish.
  • Request exact bleed, safe area and PDF submission settings.
  • Ask for accepted color and image specifications.
  • Agree on proof and revision approval workflow.

Where to verify the details

Production requirements differ among print providers. Check the specific job template and consult the BleedBench reference directory for manufacturer documentation on page boxes, bleed, effective PPI and PDF output. If a client or shop gives a different specification, obtain clarification before export.
